# BioPrint reliability benchmark

Two measurements, one real and one synthetic, both reproducible from this repo
with Node built-ins only:

| | Command | What it measures | Evidence class |
|---|---|---|---|
| CMU keystroke benchmark | `npm run benchmark` | BioPrint's passive keystroke matcher on 51 real people (20,400 recorded password entries), compared like-for-like with the best published detector on the same data | **Real human data** |
| End-to-end simulation | `npm run simulate` | The whole login pipeline (enrollment, calibration, passive decision, automation and replay gates, latency) driven by 30 synthetic human-like personas and four attack classes | **Synthetic**, pipeline behaviour only |

Outputs: `data/benchmark/cmu-results.json`, `data/benchmark/simulation-results.json`, and the tables below (regenerated by the scripts between the `<!-- ...:begin/end -->` markers).

## 1. CMU keystroke benchmark (real data)

**Dataset.** Killourhy & Maxion's DSL-StrongPasswordData: 51 subjects typed the
password `.tie5Roanl` + Return 400 times each over 8 sessions (50 per session,
sessions at least a day apart). Each repetition is 31 timing features: hold
time per key, down-down and up-down latency per key pair.

**Protocol (identical to the paper).** Enroll on a subject's first 200
repetitions, test genuine on the remaining 200, impostor = the first 5
repetitions of each of the other 50 subjects. Sweep the threshold per subject,
report each subject's equal error rate (EER) and the mean over subjects. The
paper's best detectors: scaled Manhattan 0.096, nearest-neighbor Mahalanobis
0.100, outlier-count z-score 0.102 (mean EER). Our script re-implements scaled
Manhattan in the same file so the comparison is apples to apples; it
reproduces the paper's 0.096 exactly.

**How BioPrint is scored.** Each repetition is rebuilt into the exact event
stream the browser recorder emits (`keydown`/`keyup` with `field`, `position`,
`action:'character'`, timestamps in ms from H and DD), followed by a keyboard
`submit`. It then goes through the real `analyzePassive` →
`calibratePassive` → `matchPassive` from `server/passive.js`. With a keyboard
submit and no pointer events, the only modality with evidence is the keystroke
one, so the fused identity score *is* the keystroke score; no synthetic pointer
tail was needed and nothing non-keystroke leaks in. Two variants:

- **password-field matcher**: keys hidden (privacy allowlist), so the model
  sees only medians: dwell, down-down, up-down, overlap ratio, pause ratio,
  burst length. This is what the product uses on the password.
- **username-field matcher**: keys visible, so the model also learns every
  key's hold time and every digraph latency. This is what the product uses on the username;
  here it shows what the same 10-character text buys when the keys are known.

Enrollment sizes: 200 repetitions (the paper), 10 repetitions (our
`PASSIVE_ROUNDS`, both the *first* 10 the subject ever typed and the *last* 10
of the training block), and a same-session hold-out (drift control).

### What the CMU numbers mean

**The harness is sound.** Our re-implementation of the paper's scaled Manhattan
detector reproduces the published 0.096 mean EER (± 0.069) to three decimals.

**Both BioPrint keystroke matchers beat the paper's best detector.** With 200
enrollments the username-field matcher (medians plus per-key hold time and
per-digraph latency) and the password-field matcher (medians plus per-position
hold time and press-to-press latency) both reach **8.2% mean EER against the
paper's 9.6%**; with ten enrollments taken from the end of the training block
both reach **6.0% against 8.1%**. On this corpus the two matchers see the same
information, because the password is a fixed string: "hold on the fifth
character" and "hold on the `5` key" are the same measurement. They differ only
on a real login, where the username matcher can also name the keys and the
password matcher, by design, cannot.

**Per-key hold time was the missing feature, not the fusion rule.** Before
passive-4 the username matcher learned digraph and trigraph latencies but no
per-key hold times, and scored 11.4% / 9.4%. `npm run ablate:username` shows
where the gap went: adding per-key hold time takes it to 8.6% / 6.6%, dropping
the trigraph latencies (each the sum of two digraphs already in the model, so
correlated noise) takes it to 8.2% / 6.0%, and swapping the Gaussian kernel for
a Laplace one (which makes the geometric mean rank like scaled Manhattan) moves
it by 0.3 points either way. The two fusion what-ifs below tell the same story.

**Enrollment must happen after the user is practised.** Enrolling on the very
first 10 repetitions a subject ever typed gives 27% EER for both matchers;
scaled Manhattan collapses to 23%. The subjects speed up markedly over the
first session. The last-10 rows are the fair proxy for a demo user who already
knows their password.

**Drift costs about a point.** Holding out samples from the *same* sessions as
the training data gives 9.0% against 8.2% for session-separated test data, so
what remains between BioPrint and a perfect matcher is not session-to-session
drift either.

**The score scale depends on the feature count, so both bars come from the
person's own enrollment.** The production rule (`calibratePassive`: accept at
the 10th percentile of the leave-one-round-out scores, clamped to [0.40,
0.90]) lands every one of the 51 subjects near 0.41-0.43 for both matchers, and
the EER operating point sits at 0.36-0.38 (200 reps) or 0.25-0.27 (10 reps).
Scores of 0.85+ are what the synthetic unit-test fixtures produce (uniform ±30%
jitter around fixed medians with a fixed pointer path), not what real typists
produce; the old 0.85 floor rejected 59-90% of genuine CMU logins and is gone.

## 2. End-to-end simulation (synthetic)

**This is synthetic.** It does not show how real people behave; it shows what
the real engine (`server/core.js` + `server/login.js`, SQLite on disk) does
when fed plausible human traces and plausible attacks, and how long it takes.

**Personas.** 30 users, each with parameters drawn from population ranges:
median key hold 80–140 ms, median digraph latency 120–350 ms, per-character
multipliers so the *same* person types the *same* letter pairs the same way,
15–25% per-event lognormal noise, 2–8% pause probability (0.5–1.2 s pauses),
0–4% typo-and-backspace probability, Tab vs click field transition, 250–700 ms
field transition, 150–500 ms key-to-pointer hand-over, Fitts'-law movement time
(a = 50–150 ms, b = 120–220 ms/bit) with a minimum-jerk velocity profile,
7–17 ms pointer sampling with 25% jitter, a per-user path bow, tremor,
overshoot, 60–140 ms click hold, and a preferred click spot on the button.
Every session additionally drifts 6–8%.

**Protocol.** Each persona enrolls exactly as the demo does (8 active rounds,
10 passive rounds, `completeEnrollmentFlow`), then: 20 genuine logins in fresh
sessions; every other persona attempts the account twice with the correct
password (zero-effort impostor); 6 personas moved 50% of the way toward the
victim on every parameter attempt twice (mimic); three scripted bots × 3
(synthetic `isTrusted:false` events with exact timing and a ruler-straight
sweep; the same with `isTrusted` spoofed; spoofed trust with randomised
timing); an exact resubmission of a captured genuine login, and the same
capture time-scaled ×1.03. Latency is the wall-clock time of
`core.submitPassive` (which includes the SQLite writes).

### What the simulation numbers mean

**The gates work.** All 180 exact-timing bots were rejected with
`AUTOMATION_RISK`, whether their events were `isTrusted:false` or spoofed as
trusted; the recorder-independent heuristics (uniform key timing, periodic
pointer sampling, perfect geometry, no hand-over pause) are enough on their
own. The spoofed bot with randomised timing passes the humanity gate
(humanScore 0.95) and is stopped only by identity (median score 0.13, all
STEP_UP). All 60 replays were rejected: byte-identical resubmission by the
exact signature, the ×1.03 time-scaled copy by the normalised near-replay
fingerprint. No bot or replay reached ACCEPT in either population.

**Identity separates users; the floor rejects them.** Genuine logins score a
median 0.63 (0.68 at half noise), zero-effort impostors 0.22, mimics 0.45–0.50.
Pooled EER is 7.2% against zero-effort impostors (4.0% at half noise) and
31% (21%) against a 50% mimic. But under the production rule every persona's
threshold is the 0.85 floor (the calibration asked for 0.36–0.43 on average),
so **only 3.5% of genuine logins are accepted passively (6.3% at half noise);
94–97% are stepped up**, with `PASSIVE_IDENTITY_UNCERTAIN` as the sole reason.
Impostor FAR is 0%. At a fixed 0.50 the same scores give 8–18% genuine FRR,
2–4% zero-effort FAR and 39–49% mimic FAR. This is the same picture as the real CMU data: the
matcher discriminates, the threshold floor does not let it.

**Why the unit tests pass and this does not.** `test/passive-fixtures.js`
varies every event by a uniform ±30% around fixed medians with an identical
pointer path each time, so the per-round medians barely move and scores land
above 0.85. These personas add per-character habits, pauses, corrections,
session drift and Fitts'-law pointer variability; that is enough to put the
geometric mean of ~40 feature kernels at 0.6–0.7 for the true owner.

**Which modality carries the signal (synthetic only).** Username digraphs and
cross-modal timing separate best (EER 17% / 17%, 9% / 9% at half noise);
password medians and pointer statistics are weaker (25–28%). The pointer result
says as much about the generator (every persona uses the same button layout
and path family) as about the engine; do not quote it as a property of real
users.

**Latency is not a concern.** Median 3.1 ms and p95 5.8 ms per passive
decision including all SQLite writes, max 19 ms, on one process; 3,030
decisions in 17 s.

## 3. Limitations (read these before quoting a number)

- **CMU is keystroke-only.** No pointer, click or cross-modal evidence exists
  in that dataset, so 60% of BioPrint's fusion weight (pointer 0.25, click
  0.10, cross-modal 0.15) is unvalidated on real humans. No public mouse
  dataset was benchmarked.
- **CMU is one fixed 10-character password, 51 subjects, lab conditions**,
  typed 50 times back-to-back per session. A login a day is a different
  regime; the first-10 vs last-10 rows bound the practice effect.
- **The impostors in CMU are zero-effort** (the paper's protocol), not mimics.
  The mimic numbers exist only in the synthetic run.
- **Per-subject EER is an optimistic summary**: it assumes the best threshold
  per user is known. The "production rule" rows are what a deployment would
  actually see with the engine as written.
- **Conversion choices**: Shift+r is one key (as in the paper's feature vector);
  the Return key is dropped (a navigation key never enters dwell/flight
  statistics; keeping it would only mark the capture incomplete, see
  recommendation 2).
- **The simulation is synthetic.** Every distribution in the persona model is
  asserted, not measured. It validates pipeline mechanics (gates, replay,
  calibration plumbing, latency) and corroborates the threshold finding; the
  modality ranking and the absolute accept rates should not be quoted as
  human results. Two noise levels are given to show the sensitivity.
- **Latency** is in-process `core.submitPassive` on one machine; HTTP, TLS and
  password hashing are not included.

## 4. Recommendations for the engine

Items 1-3 were open findings of the first benchmark run and are now
implemented; they are kept here with their evidence because the numbers
justify the current design.

1. **Done: the 0.85 threshold floor is gone.** On real data the floor was the
   dominant cause of false rejection: mean FRR 59-90% at 0.85 versus an EER
   operating point of 0.34-0.57. `calibratePassive` now accepts at the 10th
   percentile of the leave-one-round-out scores, clamped to [0.40, 0.90], with
   a reject bar at 0.4x the raw percentile; `docs/ALGORITHM.md` ("Three-band
   decision") has the measured comparison against `median - k·MAD` rules and
   fixed thresholds.
2. **Done: Enter-key submissions are complete captures.** `analyzePassive`
   treats a still-held navigation key on a keyboard submit as the expected
   shape of the capture, so keyboard submitters are no longer stepped up with
   `INSUFFICIENT_PASSIVE_EVIDENCE`; `test/login.test.js` covers it.
3. **Done: both fields learn per-key timing.** The privacy allowlist retains
   `position`, so the password field learns per-position hold and
   press-to-press latency without any character leaking (passive-3: 18.4% to
   8.2% EER at 200 rounds), and the username field learns per-key hold time
   alongside its digraph latencies (passive-4: 11.4% to 8.2%). Trigraph
   latencies were removed in passive-4; `npm run ablate:username` reproduces
   the ablation. Profile migration re-derives features from the retained raw
   events (`node tools/research.js migrate`).
4. **Do not enroll on a credential the user is still learning.** First-10 vs
   last-10 on CMU: 27% vs 6% EER. For the demo, have users type the
   password a few times before the counted rounds; the quarantined passive
   adaptation (`passive-adaptation.js`) can then refresh the model from
   confident logins, and its bar is now relative to the person's own accept
   threshold rather than a fixed 0.97.
5. **Mimics are a step-up problem, not a passive one.** A 50% mimic scores
   0.40-0.50 median on the synthetic population (24.5% EER, 26% FAR at a
   0.5 threshold). The passive layer should be presented as a zero-effort and bot
   filter; the active challenge remains the answer to a studied attacker.
6. **Keep the automation gate as is.** Real CMU typists tripped none of the
   UNIFORM / LOW_ENTROPY / IMPLAUSIBLE signals across 40,800 derivations, and
   both exact-timing bot classes were rejected 100% of the time. The
   randomised-timing bot shows the gate's limit: past it, identity is the
   only defence, which is another reason the threshold must sit where the
   calibration puts it.

## 5. Literature the features map to

Only mappings that are true of this codebase are claimed.

1. Killourhy, K. S. & Maxion, R. A. (2009). *Comparing Anomaly-Detection
   Algorithms for Keystroke Dynamics.* DSN 2009. The dataset and protocol used
   in section 1; the hold / down-down / up-down features are `dwell`, `flight`
   and `upDown` in `analyzePassive`; its scaled-Manhattan detector is the
   baseline in `tools/benchmark-cmu.js`.
2. Monrose, F. & Rubin, A. D. (2000). *Keystroke dynamics as a biometric for
   authentication.* Future Generation Computer Systems 16(4). Per-user
   digraph latency profiles: the `digraph:` medians (with per-key `key:x:dwell`
   hold times) the username field learns in `server/passive.js`.
3. Gamboa, H. & Fred, A. (2004). *A behavioral biometric system based on
   human-computer interaction.* SPIE 5404. Pointer stroke features (velocity,
   acceleration, curvature, straightness): `stats.pointer.velocity`,
   `acceleration`, `curvature`, `efficiency`.
4. Ahmed, A. A. E. & Traore, I. (2007). *A New Biometric Technology Based on
   Mouse Dynamics.* IEEE TDSC 4(3). Point-and-click action timing: the click
   `hold` and `arrival` (hover-before-click) statistics and `entryVelocity`.
5. Zheng, N., Paloski, A. & Wang, H. (2011). *An efficient user verification
   system via mouse movements.* CCS 2011. Angle-based metrics (direction
   change, angle of curvature): `stats.pointer.turn`, `curvature`,
   `approachAngle`, `correctionRatio`.
6. Sakoe, H. & Chiba, S. (1978). *Dynamic programming algorithm optimization
   for spoken word recognition.* IEEE TASSP 26(1). Banded DTW (`window=8`) in
   `server/matching/index.js` `dtw()`, used by the **active** exemplar matcher
   only; the passive matcher is statistical (medians, MAD-scaled kernels).
7. Bours, P. (2012). *Continuous keystroke dynamics: A different perspective
   towards biometric evaluation.* Information Security Technical Report 17.
   **Not implemented as such**: `server/continuous.js` re-scores 30-second
   windows against the ambient model and forces re-authentication when no
   strong window has been seen for 5 minutes. That is a freshness timer, not
   Bours' penalty-and-reward trust accumulator; cite it as related work only.
